A guide for teachers

Reading agency levels

Every session a student runs comes back with one level. It answers a single question — where did the student's own thinking enter this work? — and it is the first thing to read on any report.

The level measures agency, not quality. A student who confidently directed a shallow session sits higher than one who thought carefully about material the AI handed them. That is deliberate. Whether the work is any good is your judgement, made against your own rubric — the level tells you something you could not otherwise see: how the piece came to exist.

The four levels

They run in one order, lowest to highest, and they are never numbered. Each one describes the session — not the student.

Passive

The student asked the AI for answers and used them with little change.

What you'll see

  • The first message hands over the whole task.
  • Student turns are short and procedural — "make it longer", "ok", "thanks".
  • Every idea in the finished piece appears on the AI's side of the chat first.

What it doesn't mean

It is not a cheating finding. A student who didn't know where to start, or who read the task as beyond them, produces this same session. Passive tells you the thinking isn't in the work; it does not tell you whether it exists.

Worth a conversation

"What did you think about this before you opened the chat?"

If there's an answer, the thinking existed and never made it in — which is a very different problem from having none, and a much easier one to fix.

Reactive

The AI supplied the ideas, and the student developed them into their own work.

What you'll see

  • Genuine engagement — pushing back, asking for changes, questioning a claim.
  • But every student turn answers something the AI said first.
  • Trace any idea in the essay back far enough and it starts in an AI turn.

What it doesn't mean

It is not low effort. Reactive sessions are often the hardest-working ones on the list — a student can spend forty turns rigorously interrogating material and still never have set the terms of it.

Worth a conversation

"What would you have written if you'd started this on paper?"

The gap between that answer and what they handed in is the thing to talk about. Also check the assignment — see Your task may set the ceiling below.

Directive

The student brought their own ideas and used the AI to develop them.

What you'll see

  • A position, a structure or a line of argument stated by the student early.
  • AI turns that answer to terms the student set, rather than proposing their own.
  • Ideas in the finished piece that appear in a student turn before an AI one.

What it doesn't mean

It does not mean the work is good. A student can direct a session confidently toward a thin, incurious answer and still read directive. The level says the thinking was theirs, not that it was right.

Worth a conversation

"Which of your own instructions would you change now?"

Directive students often never revisit the frame they set in the first two minutes. That frame is usually where the interesting teaching is.

Transformative

The student asked the AI to test their ideas, and the ideas changed as a result.

What you'll see

  • A position stated early that is recognisably different by the end.
  • The turns where it moved are visible — you can point at them.
  • The finished piece argues something the student did not walk in with.

What it doesn't mean

It is not "argued with the AI a lot." Pushback that changes nothing is still directive. This level requires that the thinking actually moved — and moved because of work the student did, not because the AI talked them into something.

Worth a conversation

"Where did you change your mind, and what did it?"

If they can name the moment, this is the session to hold up to the class. If they can't, it's worth checking whether the change was theirs or the AI's.

Not one of the four Not enough here

There isn't enough in this session to read.

What you'll see

A handful of turns, an abandoned session, or a finished piece with almost no conversation behind it.

What it doesn't mean

It is not the bottom of the ladder, and it is not a finding about the student. Reading a three-turn transcript as passive would turn "we couldn't see it" into "you didn't do it."

Worth a conversation

This one goes straight to you, because the tool deliberately doesn't guess why. A student who worked somewhere else, a student who ran out of time, and a student who handed in something they didn't write all produce the same evidence. Only you can tell which.

How Tau decides a level

A level is never read from one chat on its own, and never from a single number.

Your task may set the ceiling

Some assignments cap the level a student can reach, and it is worth knowing which of yours do.

If you hand students an AI-written argument and ask them to evaluate it, the highest available reading is reactive — every contribution is downstream of AI material by design. A student who does that task brilliantly still comes back reactive. That is not them falling short; it is the shape of the task, and a class full of reactive sessions on a task like that is exactly right.

Open is not the same as vague. If you want more agency, leave the approach free — not the standard. An unclear task doesn't produce directive students; it produces students asking the AI what you wanted, which is the most reactive session there is. Say precisely what good looks like, and leave how they get there open.

How to talk about a level with a student

The level describes what happened in one session. It is not a label for a person, and the difference shows up in the sentence you use.

"You're a passive learner." "In this session, none of the thinking started with you." The first is a claim about who they are; the second is a claim about one afternoon, and it's the only one the tool can support.
"You only got to reactive." "Everything you worked on, the AI put in front of you first." The level is not a grade and doesn't have a pass mark. Describe what the session shows and let them see it.
"You caught the AI's mistake — good." "You turned down a claim it made." Whether the AI was actually wrong is your call, not the tool's. It reports what the student did, not who was right.

Four things the level is not

  • Not a score. There is no total, no average, and nothing out of twenty. Four levels and a name, and that's the whole scale.
  • Not calculated from the four readings underneath it. The dimensions on the report describe what a reader saw; they don't add up to the level. A session can read directive with unremarkable readings throughout, or reactive with strong ones, if what the student drove was never the part that mattered. See The four dimensions.
  • Not comparable across different assignments without care. Two classes doing different work are not on the same footing, which is why the tool won't pool them for you.
  • Not a substitute for reading the transcript. The level tells you which sessions to open first. It was never meant to save you from opening them.

The level is read from the whole session — the conversation, the finished piece, and the assignment together — by the same reading that produces the evidence beneath it. Where it lands somewhere the readings underneath wouldn't suggest, it tells you in one sentence what it read to get there, and points you at the moment in the transcript.
